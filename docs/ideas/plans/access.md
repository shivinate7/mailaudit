# Every Hand, Every Light: inclusive directions for the mail-day checklist

Lens: bodies, senses and circumstance. One thumb, tired eyes, wet hands, a tremor, a screen reader, a dim hallway. Each direction below makes the tool better for the person in the worst moment, and quietly better for everyone else.

All names and examples are invented.

## Say It Back

**Idea.** Voice-first check-off with spoken confirmation. The person says a fragment ("bolt, foil") and the tool speaks back the single best match and its seller, then marks it on a second word ("yes") or a sharp tap anywhere. Ambiguous fragments get a short spoken shortlist ("three matches: one, Kestrel Cards; two, Owlbear Singles…").
**Who and why now.** Hands full of envelopes and scissors; people with tremor or limited fine motor control; screen-reader users who today must swipe through hundreds of rows. Card names are long and punctuated ("Ashen Wyrm // Ember Rite (Showcase) – Foil"), so typing is the worst input.
**Rough shape.** A large "listen" zone (the whole lower half on the phone; one full screen on the folding phone). Fuzzy matching tolerant of dropped punctuation, set codes, "the", numerals said as words. Audio and haptic confirmation of every mark. Spoken "undo" reverses the last action.
**Risks.** Recognition errors on invented fantasy names; noisy kitchens; privacy if always listening (must be press-to-talk or explicit session). Mis-marks if confirmation is skipped.
**Owner decides.** Push-to-talk vs a timed "mail session" that listens; whether speech goes through the device's on-board recognizer only; how strict the confirmation step is.

## One Thumb, Bottom Half

**Idea.** A mail-day mode that rearranges everything so every action lives in the bottom third of the screen, reachable by the thumb of the hand holding the phone. The list scrolls from the bottom up; the current envelope's cards sit just above the thumb; marking is a big swipe or a long press rather than a small checkbox.
**Who and why now.** Anyone holding an envelope in the other hand, which is everyone on mail day. People with reduced grip or reach. On the two-screen folding phone, one screen becomes "this envelope" and the other "everything else", so the active pane is always the near one.
**Rough shape.** Targets at least the size of a fingertip pad, generous gaps, no action near screen edges where gestures conflict. Swipe right marks, and the row stays visible, dimmed, with an undo chip for several seconds rather than disappearing. No confirmation dialogs; everything undoable.
**Risks.** Accidental swipes while scrolling; left-handed users need a mirror; mode clutter if it diverges too far from the normal view.
**Owner decides.** Whether this is a separate mode or simply the default phone layout; handedness setting; how long undo lingers.

## Readable in Any Light

**Idea.** A glance-proof visual language: every state (received, outstanding, overdue, maybe lost) is told by shape and word as well as colour, and a high-legibility setting enlarges the card name and trims the noise (set code, condition, seller shrink to a second line).
**Who and why now.** Colourblind users (a red/green "arrived/missing" pairing fails about one man in twelve); tired eyes at night; glare at a sunny door; low-vision users at large text sizes where long names wrap into chaos.
**Rough shape.** Icons plus words for states (a filled circle "Here", a hollow ring "Coming", a ringed clock "Late", a warning triangle "Maybe lost"). Respect the device text size up to the largest settings without truncating the name; let rows grow. A one-tap "dark, high contrast" toggle beside the list, not buried in settings.
**Risks.** Taller rows mean more scrolling; designers may resist the extra words; testing at many text sizes is real work.
**Owner decides.** The state vocabulary and its icons; whether high legibility becomes the default; which secondary details can be hidden.

## Feel It, Hear It

**Idea.** A non-visual confirmation channel so the person can mark items without looking. A distinct haptic and a short sound per outcome: "marked", "that envelope is now complete", "this card was not in your orders", "undone".
**Who and why now.** Screen-reader and low-vision users; anyone whose eyes are on the scissors; noisy environments where sound alone fails (so haptics), quiet ones where haptics alone suffice.
**Rough shape.** A small palette of patterns, each learnable within a session. Screen reader announcements for the same events, phrased briefly ("Marked. Two left from Kestrel Cards."). Settings to mute sound, keep haptics, or both.
**Risks.** Too many patterns become noise; phones vary in haptic quality; sound annoys others in the house.
**Owner decides.** The palette size; defaults on or off; whether the "envelope complete" moment deserves something more celebratory.

## Forgive Everything

**Idea.** Replace every "are you sure" with instant action plus generous, visible undo, and keep a short recent-actions trail the person can step back through.
**Who and why now.** People with tremor who mis-tap; anyone who marked the wrong printing; screen-reader users for whom modal confirmations are slow and disorienting.
**Rough shape.** A thin strip under the list ("Marked Ashen Wyrm · Undo") that stays several seconds, plus a trail of the last ten actions reachable by one gesture. Bulk actions (mark whole envelope) undo as one step.
**Risks.** Undo can hide genuine mistakes if the strip fades too quickly; the trail must survive sync between devices.
**Owner decides.** Undo window length; how far back the trail goes; whether bulk actions get a second step.

Top pick: Say It Back — it removes the single hardest act (finding and tapping one long name among hundreds) for the widest range of bodies and circumstances, and it composes with every other direction here.

# Second pass: five more directions from the same planner

The same planner sent a second, separate report. Its directions differ enough from the first pass to keep. Its premises came from the brief, and none were checked: items are checked off one at a time, it runs in a browser and on a phone (including a two-screen folding phone), data syncs, and card names are long and full of punctuation.

## Envelope in hand
**Idea.** A one-thumb "package open" mode. Pick the sender once, and everything for that envelope sits in the bottom third of the screen.
**Who and why.** Everyone on mail day. The other hand is always busy with a torn envelope, a sleeve or a pan. It also helps people with a tremor or low grip strength.
**Shape.** Tap "Mail came" and type two or three letters of the sender ("tid" finds *Tidepool Cards*). The items in that package fill the bottom of the screen as full-width rows at least 56pt tall. A tap counts on release, and a small slip off the row still counts. A "Whole package" bar checks off everything left. No confirm dialogs: each check-in drops a chip into an undo strip ("3 checked · Undo"), which stays until the next package. The mode keeps the open package if the person walks away to stir something.
**Risks.** Sellers with near-identical names. A forgiving tap target might catch the next row.
**Owner decides.** Whether "Whole package" needs a hold or a plain tap. How long undo lasts: until the next package, the end of the day, or forever through a history list.

## Speakable names
**Idea.** Give every card one cleaned "spoken name" alongside its printed name. Screen readers, voice control, search and read-back all use it.
**Who and why.** Screen-reader users hear "Æther-Bound Wanderer, the Unmoored // Return" read out as punctuation. Voice-control users can't say it. Typists don't know which dash it uses. Everyone gets faster search.
**Shape.** The printed name stays as it is. Underneath sit the spoken form ("Aether Bound Wanderer, return side"), a short handle (set code plus number, like "XYZ 112") and plain-letter search, so "aether" finds "Æther". Rows announce state first, then name, then cost: "Outstanding. Aether Bound Wanderer. Forty cents."
**Risks.** Automatic cleaning can mangle names, so the owner needs a way to fix one. Two different printings can end up with the same spoken name.
**Owner decides.** Whether handles use set code plus number or a short invented word. Whether owner fixes to names sync.

## Counter mode on the fold
**Idea.** Half-fold the phone on the kitchen counter. The top screen is the list. The bottom screen holds three huge buttons: Got it, Not this one, Undo. Voice or a knuckle works them.
**Who and why.** Wet or floury hands, cooking. Also anyone who can't hold a phone steady for long. Unfolded, the second screen does the same job.
**Shape.** Say "next" or tap the bottom screen to step through the open package. The app reads each item aloud by its spoken name. When it can't tell which card is meant, it offers numbered choices ("one, two or three?") and you answer with a number. Every act gets a sound and a haptic: one short tick for an item, a rising pair when a package is complete, and a low double tone for "this isn't in any order you know". Nothing ever depends on sound alone.
**Risks.** Kitchen noise. Microphone permission and privacy, so speech should stay on the device. Fold postures behave differently on each phone.
**Owner decides.** Push-to-talk or always listening while the mode is open. Whether sounds are on by default.

## Readable in the dark
**Idea.** One way of showing state that works without colour, in dim light, at a glance from arm's length, and through touch.
**Who and why.** Colourblind users. Tired eyes at 11pm. A glance at the counter mid-recipe.
**Shape.** Shape, fill and word carry every state together: a filled disc for "arrived", a hollow ring for "outstanding", a notched triangle with a day count for "late, 23 days". A dim theme uses warm low glare but strong contrast. A glance band shows three big numbers: items left, money tied up, packages late. Long-press a row and you feel its state: one buzz for arrived, two for outstanding, three for late.
**Risks.** Haptic patterns are hard to learn, so they need a key. Big numbers take room from the list.
**Owner decides.** Whether the dim theme follows the system or the time of day. Which state wins when a package is both partly arrived and late.

## The envelope reads itself
**Idea.** Point the camera at the return address or packing slip. The phone reads it on the device and suggests the matching package. It never chooses for you.
**Who and why.** Low-vision users. People who can't type well. Anyone with a stack of twenty envelopes.
**Shape.** A camera button in the "Mail came" mode. The match shows as a suggestion, *Tidepool Cards, 4 items*, with Accept and Not this one. If nothing matches, the envelope goes to the no-sender pile untouched. The photo is thrown away after reading unless the owner keeps it.
**Risks.** The envelope carries the owner's home address, so nothing should leave the phone. Handwritten labels will read badly. A wrong suggestion accepted on autopilot.
**Owner decides.** Whether to keep the image at all. How close a match must be before it is suggested.

Top pick (second pass): Speakable names — it is the foundation that voice, screen reader, search, counter mode and the camera match all reuse, and it makes everyday search faster even for someone who never turns on an assistive feature.

# Phase 2: Say It Back, fleshed out (with Speakable names as its base)

The app says each check-in back in a clean, speakable form. Later, the person can also speak a check-in. Both use one name layer per product: the spoken name, a short handle and a plain-letter search form.

**How it fits what exists.**
- Undo goes through the existing recent-checks log; no second undo strip. "Undo" spoken or tapped reverses the newest entry.
- Voice can file an envelope into the orphaned pile, but never moves it out to a seller; that takes touch.
- The lost-mail warning and the per-product cost total are only read aloud ("Last of four. Package complete. Two forty spent"). Their logic does not change.
- Stamps and photos are out of scope at first.
- Orders from the browser extension get spoken names by the same rule as CSV imports.
- The read-back caption sits in a fixed-height slot that is always reserved, so nothing moves under the pointer. No native dialogs. Open question: does the browser's own microphone-permission prompt count under the no-native-dialogs rule? One reason voice input is the second slice.

**Flow**
1. Mail arrives. The person opens Packages and taps the sender, or types "tid".
2. They tap an item's row, as today.
3. The app speaks and captions: "Got it. Aether Bound Wanderer. One of four from Tidepool." A short haptic on the phone. A screen reader hears the same line through a polite announcement.
4. Last item in the package: "Package complete." A product across sellers: "Three of four arrived across sellers."
5. Wrong tap: say or tap "Undo". The app says back what it reversed.
6. With voice on (slice 2), hold the mic button and say "Wanderer" or "XYZ 112". One match: checked and said back. Several: numbered in the caption; say or tap a number. None: "Not found. Keep it, or send to orphaned?"
7. Done when the package is complete. The recent-checks log holds every act.

**Screen states for a mockup**
- A Packages row just checked, caption below the list: "Got it. Aether Bound Wanderer, return side. 1 of 4 from Tidepool Cards. Undo".
- Voice listening on the phone: held mic button in the thumb zone; caption "Listening… say a name or a number like XYZ 112".
- Several matches: "1 Wanderer, the Unmoored (XYZ 112) · 2 Wanderer's Return (XYZ 113)" with two big number targets.
- Fold half-folded on the counter: list on the top screen; Got it, Next and Undo on the bottom, last read-back in large type.

**Data**
- Spoken name: made at import from the product name — strip punctuation, fold "Æ" to "AE", turn "//" into "return side". Per product, shared by Tally and Packages.
- Handle: set code plus collector number if the export carries them (unknown); otherwise a short unique word.
- Owner fixes to a spoken name: a per-product override that syncs. Needs a new field, and the saved-state shape is fixed, so this needs an owner ruling.
- Voice: the speech recognition and synthesis built into each platform. Privacy: some browsers send audio to a server; iPhone can recognise on the device.

**Smallest first slice.** Spoken names plus read-back only: caption, speech on/off, screen-reader announcement and haptic, undo through the existing log. No microphone, no new stored field; names are worked out at load time, so no saved state changes.

**Owner decisions**
- Speech on by default? Recommend: caption always on, speech off until turned on once per device.
- Store owner fixes to spoken names? Recommend: yes, in slice 2, after a ruling on the saved-state shape.
- Voice input on the web? Recommend: iPhone and fold first, on-device speech only.
- Push-to-talk or open mic? Recommend: push-to-talk; open mic only in counter mode on the fold.
- Can voice send mail to the orphaned pile? Recommend: yes. Only touch takes it back out.
